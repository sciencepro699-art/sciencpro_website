Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

Turn a current on and watch compasses move. Build a solenoid and use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of force.

1 · Current makes a magnet

Switch the current on and see the compass needles turn around the wire. Reverse the current and the field reverses. Hold your right thumb along the current and your curled fingers show the field.

Current: 3 A

2 · Solenoid

A solenoid acts like a bar magnet. Look at the current at each end: clockwise is the south pole, anticlockwise is the north pole.

Turns: 10
Current: 2 A

3 · Force on a wire and the motor

Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion (F-C-M). Choose the field and the current and read the direction of force.

Current: 2 A

Oersted's experiment

A current in a wire deflects a nearby compass needle. So a current produces a magnetic field around the wire. Reverse the current and the needle turns the other way.

current → magnetic field

Right-hand thumb rule

Hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb along the current. The curled fingers point along the field lines.

thumb: current · fingers: field

Solenoid

A coil of many turns behaves like a bar magnet. The end where the current is anticlockwise is the north pole. Clockwise is the south pole.

anticlockwise N · clockwise S

Stronger electromagnet

Increase the number of turns per unit length, increase the current, or put a soft iron core inside. Soft iron loses its magnetism when the current stops.

more turns · more current · iron core

Force on a conductor

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a force. It is largest when the wire is at right angles to the field. Reverse the current or the field and the force reverses.

F-C-M: Field, Current, Motion

Electric motor

A motor changes electric energy into mechanical energy. The forces on the two sides of the coil are opposite, so the coil turns. A split-ring commutator reverses the current every half turn, so the coil keeps turning one way.

electrical → mechanical

Loudspeaker

A moving-coil loudspeaker has a coil in a magnetic field. The changing current from the signal moves the coil and the cone, and this makes sound.

varying current → vibrating cone
