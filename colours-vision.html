Colours and Vision

Mix red, green and blue lights, see objects in coloured light, split white light with a prism and fix the eye defects with lenses.

1 · Mixing coloured lights

Switch the primary colours of light on and off. Red, green and blue together make white. Two primaries make a secondary colour.

2 · Objects in coloured light

An opaque object reflects only its own colour and absorbs the rest. Pick an object and a light and see what colour it looks.

Object colour: Red
Light colour: White

3 · Prism and dispersion

A prism splits white light into seven colours. Red bends the least and violet bends the most.

Angle of the prism: 60°

4 · Defects of the eye

Choose the eye and the object distance, then try a corrective lens. The image must fall exactly on the retina to look sharp.

Dispersion

White light splits into VIBGYOR when it passes through a prism, because each colour bends by a different amount. Red bends least and has the longest wavelength. Violet bends most. A rainbow forms by dispersion in water drops.

V I B G Y O R

Primary and secondary

Primary colours of light are red, green and blue. Red + green = yellow. Green + blue = cyan. Red + blue = magenta. All three make white.

R + G + B = white

Complementary colours

Two colours that make white light when added are complementary: red and cyan, green and magenta, blue and yellow.

R + C = G + M = B + Y = white

Scattering

Particles of the air scatter light in all directions. Blue is scattered the most and red the least. So the sky is blue. At sunrise and sunset the light travels a long way through the air, the blue is scattered away and the Sun looks red.

blue scatters most · red least

Tyndall effect

The path of light becomes visible in a colloid, such as sunlight through mist, smoke or the branches of a wood. This is scattering by the particles of the medium.

visible beam in a colloid

Myopia and hypermetropia

Short-sightedness (myopia): far objects are blurred because the image forms in front of the retina. It is corrected with a concave lens. Long-sightedness (hypermetropia): near objects are blurred because the image forms behind the retina and the near point is more than 25 cm. It is corrected with a convex lens.

myopia: concave · hypermetropia: convex

Persistence of vision

An image stays in the eye for about 1/16 of a second after the object is gone. A Newton's colour disc spun fast looks white because the colours blend. A movie looks continuous for the same reason.

image stays ≈ 1/16 s

Power of a lens

The power of a lens is 1/f, with f in metres, measured in dioptre (D). A convex lens has a positive power and a concave lens has a negative power, so a prescription of −1 D is a concave lens for myopia.

P = 1 ÷ f
