Periodic Table and Electron Configuration

Pick an element and watch its electrons fill the subshells. Then find its block, period and group.

1 · Fill the electrons

Choose an element. Its electrons fill the subshells from the lowest energy upwards. The numbers in brackets are (n + l).

Atomic number: 11

2 · Shells, subshells and orbitals

Change the shell number n and see its subshells and orbitals.

3 · Ionisation enthalpy

The energy needed to remove the outermost electron. See how it changes along a period and down a group.

Orbitals and quantum numbers

An orbital is the region around the nucleus where an electron is most likely to be found. Quantum numbers describe it: n for the shell, l for the subshell and m for the orientation.

n = 1, 2, 3, 4 … (K, L, M, N)

Subshells

The value of l runs from 0 to n − 1. l = 0 is s, 1 is p, 2 is d and 3 is f. A subshell has 2l + 1 orbitals: s has 1, p has 3, d has 5 and f has 7.

s = 2 · p = 6 · d = 10 · f = 14 electrons

Capacity

A shell has n² orbitals and holds 2n² electrons. Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons.

orbitals n² · electrons 2n²

The (n + l) rule

Subshells fill in increasing order of (n + l). If two have the same value, the one with the smaller n fills first. So 4s fills before 3d.

1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p

Writing the configuration

Write each subshell with its electron count, for example sodium is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. A short form uses the noble gas before it, so sodium is [Ne] 3s¹.

Na = [Ne] 3s¹

Chromium and copper

Half filled (d⁵) and completely filled (d¹⁰) d subshells are extra stable. So Cr is 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, not 3d⁴ 4s² or 3d⁹ 4s².

Cr: 3d⁵4s¹ · Cu: 3d¹⁰4s¹

Block, period and group

Block: the subshell that gets the last electron. Period: the highest shell number. Group: s block = outer electrons; p block = 10 + outer electrons; d block = (n−1)d electrons + ns electrons.

s: 1–2 · d: 3–12 · p: 13–18

s, p and d block elements

s block elements give up electrons and show +1 or +2. p block elements show both positive and negative oxidation states. d block (transition) elements show variable oxidation states, like Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.

Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺

Ionisation enthalpy

The minimum energy to remove the most loosely held electron from a gaseous atom. It decreases down a group and generally increases along a period.

down ↓ decreases · across → increases
